# Ph09 Waves and Oscillations

> [[MOC-physics|↑ Physics MOC]] · [[Ph09 Waves and Oscillations - Worksheet|✏️ Worksheet]] · [[Ph09 Waves and Oscillations - Solutions|✅ Solutions]] · [[Mistakes - Physics|⚠️ Mistake log]]

## Why this matters
Brain activity is full of rhythms, from slow sleep waves to fast gamma oscillations. Analysing EEG, MEG and LFP signals means decomposing them into frequencies (Fourier analysis) and filtering them. Neurons themselves act as filters: the membrane is a low-pass filter, and some neurons resonate at preferred frequencies.

## Describing an oscillation
$$x(t) = A\sin(2\pi ft + \varphi) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$$
| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amplitude | $A$ | e.g. µV | maximum deviation (half peak-to-peak) |
| Frequency | $f$ | Hz = 1/s | cycles per second |
| Period | $T = 1/f$ | s | duration of one cycle |
| Angular frequency | $\omega = 2\pi f$ | rad/s | – |
| Phase | $\varphi$ | rad or ° | position in the cycle; a lag $\Delta t$ corresponds to $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi f\Delta t$ |

**Harmonic oscillator** (mass on a spring, LC circuit): restoring force ∝ displacement, so the motion is a sine with natural frequency $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$. **Damping** makes the amplitude decay exponentially.

**Waves** are oscillations that travel in space: $v = \lambda f$ (speed = wavelength × frequency). Examples: sound (≈ 343 m/s in air, ≈ 1500 m/s in tissue; ultrasound imaging), light ([[Ph11 Optics and Fluorescence]]), and travelling waves of activity across cortex.

## Sinusoidal signals and Fourier decomposition
**Fourier's theorem:** every (reasonable) signal can be written as a **sum of sine waves** of different frequencies, amplitudes and phases:
$$x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos(2\pi nf_0t) + b_n\sin(2\pi nf_0t)\right]$$
- The **Fourier transform** gives, for each frequency $f$, a complex coefficient $X(f) = \lvert X\rvert e^{i\varphi}$: the **amplitude** and the **phase** of that component ([[E2 Complex Numbers]]).
- **Power spectrum** $P(f) = \lvert X(f)\rvert^2$: how much variance (power) is at each frequency.
- A square wave = fundamental + odd harmonics (1/3 of 3f, 1/5 of 5f, …). Sharp edges need high frequencies.
- **Frequency resolution:** a window of length $T_\text{window}$ resolves frequencies $\Delta f = 1/T_\text{window}$. 2 s of data give 0.5 Hz resolution. This is the **time–frequency trade-off**: good frequency resolution needs long windows (poor time resolution). Wavelets and short-time Fourier transforms balance the two.
- **Sampling theorem (Nyquist):** to capture frequencies up to $f_\text{max}$, sample at $f_s > 2f_\text{max}$. Otherwise **aliasing** occurs: high frequencies masquerade as low ones. Analogue anti-aliasing filters are applied before digitisation.

## Neural oscillations
| Band | Frequency (approx.) | Typical context |
|---|---|---|
| **Delta** δ | 0.5–4 Hz | deep (slow-wave) sleep, slow oscillations (< 1 Hz) |
| **Theta** θ | 4–8 Hz | hippocampus during navigation and memory; frontal midline in working memory |
| **Alpha** α | 8–13 Hz | relaxed wakefulness, eyes closed (posterior); attentional inhibition |
| **Beta** β | 13–30 Hz | motor system (desynchronises during movement); elevated in Parkinson's disease |
| **Gamma** γ | 30–100+ Hz | local processing, attention, binding; generated by PV interneuron networks |
| (Ripples) | 150–250 Hz | hippocampal sharp-wave ripples, memory replay |

- **Generation:** interactions of excitatory and inhibitory neurons (E–I loops), intrinsic resonances, thalamocortical loops.
- **Analysis:**
  - power in bands
  - **event-related (de)synchronisation**
  - phase locking of spikes to oscillations
  - cross-frequency coupling (theta phase modulates gamma amplitude)
  - coherence between areas
- **Aperiodic background:** the EEG spectrum also has a "1/f" component, $P\propto f^{-\beta}$, which must be separated from true oscillatory peaks ([[A1 Exponent Rules]]).
- **Line noise:** 50 Hz (Europe) or 60 Hz (USA) from mains power; removed with notch filters.

## Resonance and filtering
**Resonance:** a system driven at its natural frequency responds with maximal amplitude. The sharper the resonance, the less damping.

**Filters** let some frequencies pass and attenuate others:
| Filter | Passes | Electrical prototype | Cutoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low-pass** | slow components | R in series, C to ground (RC) | $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$ |
| **High-pass** | fast components | C in series, R to ground | $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$ |
| **Band-pass** | a band around $f_0$ | combination, or a resonant circuit | – |
| Notch (band-stop) | everything except a narrow band | – | e.g. 50 Hz |

- At the cutoff, amplitude is reduced to $1/\sqrt2\approx71\%$ (−3 dB). A first-order filter then falls off by −20 dB per decade (a factor 10 in amplitude per factor 10 in frequency).

**The membrane is a low-pass filter.** A passive membrane (RC) with $\tau = 20$ ms has $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\cdot0.02\,\text{s}}\approx8$ Hz. Fast input fluctuations are smoothed; slow inputs pass. Dendrites filter more strongly with distance ([[Ph10 Signal Propagation and Cable Theory]]).

**Neuronal resonance (band-pass):** some neurons express slow currents that oppose voltage changes, such as **I_h** (HCN channels) and the M-current. Combined with the low-pass membrane, this creates a **resonance** at preferred frequencies (often theta, 4–10 Hz), e.g. in hippocampal pyramidal cells and entorhinal stellate cells. Such neurons respond best to inputs at their resonant frequency.

**Recording filters:** spikes are high-pass filtered (> 300 Hz), LFP low-pass filtered (< 300 Hz), EEG band-passed (e.g. 0.1–100 Hz). AC coupling is a high-pass filter. Filters can distort waveforms and phases; zero-phase (forward-backward) digital filtering avoids phase shifts.

## Key takeaways
- $x = A\sin(2\pi ft + \varphi)$; $T = 1/f$; a lag $\Delta t$ gives a phase $2\pi f\Delta t$.
- Fourier: any signal is a sum of sines; power spectrum $= \lvert X(f)\rvert^2$; $\Delta f = 1/T_\text{window}$; sample at $> 2f_\text{max}$.
- Bands: δ (0.5–4), θ (4–8), α (8–13), β (13–30), γ (30–100+ Hz).
- RC gives a low-pass filter with $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$; the membrane is a low-pass filter (~8 Hz for τ = 20 ms); I_h can add resonance.
